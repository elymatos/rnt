## 🧠 What Is the Tolman-Eichenbaum Machine (TEM)?

The **TEM** (Whittington et al., 2020, _Cell_) is a computational model of the hippocampus that:

- **Unifies spatial and relational memory**
    
- Bridges between **Tolman’s cognitive map** and **Eichenbaum’s relational memory theory**
    
- Based on **predictive representation** of future states
    
- Uses **successor representations** and **recurrent networks** to encode **latent structure** in tasks (e.g., spatial layout, sequences, relations)
    

> 🧩 In essence, it learns **predictive relationships** between stimuli or events in a space (physical or abstract).

---

## 🧬 Key Ideas of TEM

 
|Concept|Function in TEM|
|---|---|
|**Grid-like codes**|Represent transitions between states|
|**Place-like codes**|Encode current or goal location|
|**Successor representation (SR)**|Predict future occupancy or outcomes|
|**Latent map learning**|Builds internal cognitive structure (space, abstract graph)|
|**Entorhinal input**|Provides basis functions for relational modeling|

---

## 🔁 Predictive Coding in Cortical Columns

Meanwhile, **predictive coding in cortical columns**:

- Encodes **bottom-up sensory input**
    
- Combines it with **top-down predictions** (via apical dendrites)
    
- Uses **prediction error** (via local interneuron gating) to update synapses
    
- Learns **temporal and contextual structure**
    
- Organizes **hierarchical models of sensory and motor experience**
    

---

## 🧠 Core Functional Parallel

 
|Cortical Columns (Predictive Coding)|Hippocampus (TEM)|
|---|---|
|Predict incoming sensory input|Predict future relational state|
|Integrate top-down + bottom-up|Integrate latent context + observed location|
|Minimize prediction error|Minimize trajectory error|
|Learn transition dynamics via STDP|Learn successor relationships via SR|
|Columns as units of sequence learning|Hippocampal states as relational positions|
|Recurrent local layers (2/3, 5)|Recurrent structure via MEC → CA3|

---

## 🔄 Bridging the Two

TEM can be seen as a **specialized instantiation** of **predictive processing** in the **domain of relational memory** and **navigation**.  
In other words:

> TEM = Predictive Coding Model of the Hippocampal System  
> Cortical Columns = Predictive Coding Models of Sensory and Motor Systems

They share the **same core architecture**, just adapted to different **types of input**:

- Cortex: fast, layered feedforward+feedback sensory prediction
    
- Hippocampus: structured, relational, transition-based inference over space and time
    

---

- The **hippocampus** uses these inputs to model **what comes next** across **space, episodes, or abstract tasks**.
    
- This is **high-order predictive coding**, using **successor dynamics** rather than simple input reconstruction.

Let’s unpack the contrast and connection:

---

## 🧠 Classical Predictive Coding (Cortex)

In most cortical models (especially sensory cortex):

> The brain tries to **predict incoming sensory input**, and **minimize prediction error** at each level of the hierarchy.

### What’s being predicted?

- The **next sensory input** (e.g., next frame of a visual scene, the next phoneme in speech)
    
- Prediction errors arise from **mismatch between actual input and expectation**
    
- Used for:
    
    - **Perceptual inference**
        
    - **Attention allocation**
        
    - **Learning input structure**
        

### Example:

A V1 column predicts a certain orientation of light will appear based on context and past input. If it doesn’t, prediction error rises and is passed up the hierarchy.

---

## 🧭 High-Order Predictive Coding (TEM and Beyond)

In **the hippocampus (as modeled by TEM)**, the prediction task is **qualitatively different**.

> The system predicts **what state it will be in in the future**, not what input it will see now.

This means:

- Prediction is about **what happens next**, not what is currently sensed.
    
- Prediction is about **abstract relational structure**, not just pixels or features.
    
- Learning is about **transition structure**, not just input reconstruction.
    

---

## 🔄 Successor Representations (SR): The Key Shift

In TEM, the core computational unit is the **successor representation** (SR):

> **SR(x)** = Expected **future occupancy** of other states, given that you’re currently in state **x**.

It captures:

- **Temporal relationships**
    
- **Path structure**
    
- **Latent space dynamics**
    

Instead of learning "What is this?", the system learns:

> “Given I’m here, what states will I likely visit next — and how soon?”

---

### 🧠 Why This Is High-Order

Because:

- It generalizes over **time and space**.
    
- It allows **inference and planning**: “What happens if I go left?”
    
- It’s **abstract**: SR can be used in **physical space**, **task space**, or **semantic space**.
    
- It supports **latent knowledge generalization**: using prior transition knowledge to make new inferences.
    

---

## 🔬 Comparison: Reconstruction vs Successor Prediction

  
|Feature|Classical Predictive Coding|High-Order Prediction (TEM/SR)|
|---|---|---|
|Goal|Predict current input|Predict future state probabilities|
|Focus|Sensory reconstruction|Relational dynamics and transitions|
|Learning signal|Prediction error at current step|Expected future occupancy|
|Representation|Feature codes|Latent maps, relational embeddings|
|Use case|Perception, recognition|Planning, inference, abstraction|

Imagine a rat in a maze:

> In one, prediction is about **input**.  
> In the other, prediction is about **structure** — **what comes next** in the cognitive map.

- This allows **inference**, **flexible behavior**, and **task switching**.

- Encode and generalize **relational knowledge** (e.g., spatial, semantic, social),
    
- And perform **inference** about unseen or novel combinations of known elements.
    

It’s named after:

- **Edward Tolman**: proposed the idea of **cognitive maps** in rats.
    
- **Howard Eichenbaum**: emphasized the hippocampus as a system for **relational memory**, not just space.
    

---

## 🧩 TEM: Core Idea in 1 Sentence

> TEM learns a **latent graph of entities and relationships** by modeling **transitions** between states, and it uses this to make **predictions and generalizations** in structured environments.

---

## 🔧 Components of TEM

Here’s a simplified version of its core components:

  
|Component|Role in TEM|Analog in brain|
|---|---|---|
|**Observations**|External stimuli or inputs (e.g., image of an object)|Sensory input (cortex)|
|**Latent States**|Internal representation of object identity, position, etc.|Hippocampal codes|
|**Transition Function**|Learns how states transition into one another (graph edges)|CA3 → CA1 dynamics|
|**Successor Representation (SR)**|Predicts future occupancy of latent states|Grid cells, predictive coding|
|**Generalization Layer**|Learns a mapping from latent features to novel situations|mPFC, entorhinal cortex|
|**Inference Mechanism**|Allows one-shot inference from known relationships|Hippocampal replay, maps|

---

## 🧠 What Are the "Concepts" in TEM?

In the TEM framework:

- A **concept** is a **latent vector or node** in a learned relational graph.
    
- These are **not raw words or images**, but compressed representations (like embeddings).
    
- For example:
    
    - `"dog"` and `"cat"` may be two nodes.
        
    - `"chased"` is a learned **transition** between them.
        

The system **doesn't explicitly label** a relationship as "chase" — instead, it **learns the structure of transitions** between concepts based on observed examples.

---

## 🔁 How Does TEM Encode Relations?

### It uses:

1. **A state encoder** to transform observations into latent representations.
    
2. **A transition model** to estimate probabilities over next states given the current one.
    
3. **A successor representation matrix**, where: SR(st)=∑τ=0∞γτP(st+τ∣st)\text{SR}(s_t) = \sum_{\tau = 0}^\infty \gamma^\tau P(s_{t+\tau} \mid s_t) This encodes **how often a future state will be visited** starting from the current one.
    

This means:  
→ You don’t just learn “A leads to B” —  
→ You learn “If I’m in A, I’ll likely reach B, C, and D with certain probabilities.”

---

## 📈 Learning in TEM

TEM learns from sequences of observed states:

- Sees: `[A → B → C]`
    
- Learns: transitions between states + latent features that explain them
    
- Later: Can **infer missing links** or **compose new paths**:
    
    - e.g., If “A → B” and “B → C”, infer “A → C”
        

This generalizes from navigation (places) to reasoning (entities, concepts).

TEM explains:

- How the brain supports **flexible generalization**
    
- Why **grid cells** appear in both spatial and conceptual tasks
    
- How hippocampus builds maps not just of places, but of **relational structure**
    
- Why replay and path integration are useful even in abstract reasoning

---

## ✅ Short Answer

> **Yes**, lateral connections from **layer 2/3 pyramidal neurons** are **primarily excitatory**, and **they do project across cortical columns**.  
> So yes — **layer 2/3 of one column can "know about" and excite layer 2/3 in nearby columns**.

---

## 🔬 Biological Summary

- **Layer 2/3 pyramidal cells** are **excitatory (glutamatergic)**.
    
- They project **horizontally (laterally)** across the cortical sheet, often across **multiple columns** (100s of µm to mm).
    
- These projections **target both**:
    
    - Other **pyramidal cells** (excitation → associative binding, sequence propagation)
        
    - **Interneurons**, especially **somatostatin+ or PV+ interneurons** (indirect inhibition = lateral inhibition)
        

---

## 🧠 Functional Implications

  
|Function|Excitatory or Inhibitory|Description|
|---|---|---|
|**Horizontal integration**|✅ Excitatory|One L2/3 pyramidal excites another in adjacent column|
|**Contextual modulation**|✅ Excitatory|Integrates information across nearby receptive fields|
|**Lateral inhibition**|✅ Indirect via interneurons|Suppresses competing representations|
|**Pattern association**|✅ Excitatory|Helps bind co-activated features (like edges into contours)|

---

## 📏 Range of Projections

- Can span up to **1–2 mm** horizontally
    
- Sometimes form **patchy, clustered axon terminals**
    
- This supports **structured lateral connectivity**, not just random mixing
    

Example:

- A neuron responding to a vertical bar at one location might excite neurons in neighboring columns responding to **aligned features**, aiding **contour integration** or **pattern completion**.
    

---

## 🧬 What About Inhibition?

- **Inhibition is local and lateral**, but often arises **indirectly** via:
    
    - Activation of **inhibitory interneurons** (like basket cells or Martinotti cells)
        
    - These are **recruited by L2/3 pyramidal cell activity**
        

So lateral connections in L2/3 can create:

- **Long-range excitation**
    
- **Center-surround inhibition** via interneuron circuits
    

---

## ✅ Summary

 
|Feature|Layer 2/3 lateral connections|
|---|---|
|Cell type|Excitatory pyramidal neurons|
|Connection type|Long-range horizontal axons|
|Targets|Other L2/3 pyramidal neurons, and inhibitory interneurons|
|Functional role|Pattern completion, context propagation, competition|
|Cross-column communication?|✅ Yes|
